01 · The challenge

Corporate learning sits next to the work, not inside it

The client had already built Syntea, an AI learning assistant running on Azure OpenAI that had supported its online students since October 2023 and was reachable inside Microsoft Teams. The next step was the workplace. In our kickoff they shared what they kept hearing from corporate L&D teams: training is disconnected from daily tasks, engagement is low, nobody can show what a learning programme changed, and the gap between employees’ skills and what organisations need keeps widening.

Their hypothesis was ambitious. If learning were embedded in the tasks people already do, guided by AI and tied to the company’s OKRs, it would happen implicitly and stay relevant. The brief to our team was open ended: find where Syntea could create real value for employees first, and prove it in Microsoft Teams, where the client’s corporate customers already spend their day.

Pillar 1

Implicit learning

Learning happens through real tasks and situations, guided by AI.

Pillar 2

Continuous feedback

Regular, context specific feedback keeps people aware of their progress.

Pillar 3

Competency measurement

Ongoing interactions and assessments show what actually improved.

The client’s three pillar hypothesis, paraphrased from the kickoff deck.

How might we help a new employee become productive faster, without adding more work for the managers and colleagues who onboard them?

The question we arrived at after four weeks of discovery. How we got there is below.

03 · Discover · weeks 1 to 4

Who hurts most when someone new joins?

Research roadmap

Before any interview, we ran a research roadmap canvas: what we already knew about the market, what we assumed about the audience, and which assumptions were both critical and unproven. Desk research covered corporate learning trend reports, AI mentorship literature and the tools employees already use in Teams and LinkedIn Learning.

Three hypotheses came out of it, one per target group. The pain lives with the employer who trains a new person, with the new hire being onboarded, or with the employee trying to upskill on the job.

Bull’s Eye problem interviews

Each of us interviewed people from our own networks and the client’s side: new joiners, onboarding managers, trainers and consultants who had joined large firms recently. We each built a Bull’s Eye, then merged all four into one master board.

The overlap was striking. Across industries, the pains clustered near the centre for the same two groups: new hires who can’t find answers, and managers who don’t have time to give them. Upskilling as a separate pain sat further out. People cared about learning only once they knew what their manager expected of them.

Pain point mapping

I clustered every sticky from the master Bull’s Eye into an affinity map. Six themes held up. Quotes are lightly edited and stripped of company names.

Asking questions and getting answers

My colleagues are too busy, so my questions about both internal and theoretical information get answered after 3 days.When I ask a question, most of the time I don’t get a full answer. So I have to ask more than one colleague and merge the answers.

Lack of onboarding guidance

The onboarding was the manager rambling for 40 minutes. I wasn’t sure how my role connected to it.If I had a question on a project, I’d ask another consultant, but it was the blind leading the blind.

Lack of onboarding documentation

I just needed something in writing, visuals, something I could refer to.The training repository was for the entire global firm. Huge, and hard to navigate.

Internal politics and implicit knowledge

Interpersonal advice was the most useful.Getting me aware of the social side, the bureaucracy of how a job is done.

Training takes managers’ time

The training team was constantly hunting people down to see how trainees were doing.It falls on you to do a training well, so the people you’re training can pick it up immediately.

Company concerns over AI

ChatGPT is blocked at work because of privacy concerns.Whenever I ask Copilot a complex question I get an inaccurate answer, so I don’t trust it for complex questions.
The real cost of onboarding is social. New hires stay quiet so they don’t “bother” busy colleagues, and the knowledge they need lives in people’s heads, not in documents.

05 · Built on Microsoft Copilot

Teams is the face. Copilot is the brain.

We built the agent on Microsoft Copilot, with Microsoft Teams as the only interface people see. That meant designing for two things at once: a Teams app people already know how to use, and an AI that answers from the company’s own content instead of the open web.

The client had already announced plans to bring Syntea into Microsoft 365 Copilot, so building on Copilot kept our work on the same path as their product.

01 Interface

Microsoft Teams

Chat, Adaptive Cards, suggested replies, View prompts and proactive messages. Light mode, 2024.

02 Intelligence

Microsoft Copilot

Understands the question, plans the reply and writes it in Syntea’s voice.

03 Grounding

Company knowledge

Playbooks, policies, OKRs and role descriptions from SharePoint and team files, cited in every answer.

04 People

Org and experts

Role, manager and team from the Teams profile, plus who knows what, so a question can go to a person.

What designing for Copilot meant

Tenant boundariesThe agent only sees what the person already has access to in Microsoft 365

What it cost me

The platform moved under us. During the batch Microsoft renamed Copilot extensions to Copilot agents and took them to general availability in October 2024. Copilot access in the client’s tenant had to be requested through IT. I designed to the patterns that stay stable in Teams and kept the conversation independent of any one Copilot feature.

What testing taught me

We tested Copilot on real learning material before designing around it, including having it build a deck from a corporate learning trends report. One interviewee had stopped trusting Copilot after inaccurate answers to complex questions. So every answer shows its source, and the agent hands off instead of guessing.

Keeping it safe

Several interviewees said ChatGPT was blocked at work over privacy. Building on Copilot inside the company’s own Microsoft 365 tenant answered that concern. The agent never scores or evaluates employees.

How research shaped each decision

Every direction started as an assumption. I wrote it down, checked it against interviews, desk research and the platform documentation, and only designed around it once the evidence held. These are the research principles I worked by, and the log of what each one led to.

Separate what we know from what we assumeThe research roadmap canvas split our board into existing knowledge, assumptions about the audience, and open questions, before anyone proposed a feature.
One hypothesis per target groupIs the pain with the employer who trains, the new hire being onboarded, or the employee upskilling on the job? Each hypothesis got its own interviews and its own Bull’s Eye.
Test the riskiest assumption firstWe plotted assumptions by how critical they were and how much evidence we had. Critical and unproven went to the front of the queue.
Triangulate before decidingNo decision rested on one source. Interviews, desk research, hands on competitor testing and platform docs had to point the same way.
Fact check the platform and the rulesWhat a Teams bot and Copilot could really do in 2024, and what the EU AI Act and GDPR allow, came from primary sources, not from what we hoped.
Fake it before building itWhere evidence was thin, the cheapest experiment came first: a person answering in Slack before a single prompt was written.

07 · Build and test · weeks 6 to 8

Three experiments, each cheaper than the next would have been

Experiment 1 · Wizard of Oz in Slack

Before writing a prompt, we faked the bot. Testers messaged “Onboardly” in our team’s Slack while one of us answered by hand. It tested the riskiest assumption cheaply: would people ask a bot the questions they won’t ask colleagues?

Tester
I also just found out I need to track my work time. Do you know where I’ll find the tool?
Onboardly
Yes, you can track your work time using the Time Tracker tool in the company’s internal portal, under “Productivity”…
Tester
Ok, so is there a URL or a name for this tool? Where can I find it?

Validated Testers asked practical, everyday questions without hesitation, the same kind interviewees said they avoided asking busy colleagues.

Learned A correct answer without a link is still a dead end. From then on, every answer ends with the exact place to go and the source it came from.

Experiment 2 · A real bot in Teams

Our engineers stood up a working agent in the client’s Teams tenant, with Microsoft Copilot as its intelligence and our own services keeping conversation memory and profile, OKR and goal data. I designed its flows and copy, and tested it with Teams accounts and sample company information.

Teams clientChat, cards, proactive messages
Teams appRegistered in the Teams Dev Portal, with single sign on
Microsoft CopilotUnderstands each request and writes the reply, one prompt per intent
MemorySession memory, plus people, OKRs and goals
KnowledgeManager uploads: PDFs, docs, links

The first build worked technically and failed as a conversation. Its profile agent opened by asking for nine fields in one message, so the bot asked for work before it had offered anything in return. Fixing that first turn shaped everything after it.

Design critique

“For a click dummy like this I need a context, so I, as a user, can go through it by myself. And having the hotspot flash active, the user clicks on it because it’s visible. That can be misleading.”

A senior designer at DPS. We added a scenario brief and removed hotspot hints before the next round.

What we changed for the next round

  • Show which document each part of a plan came from, so a manager can trust it.
  • Let managers edit by message (“remove the webinar”) and change only that part.
  • Give new hires one day at a time in chat, not a six month spreadsheet.

Trust, privacy and the EU AI Act

“ChatGPT is blocked at work” came up in our interviews, and DPS ran a legal workshop on GDPR and the EU AI Act during the batch. AI used for employee evaluation can count as high risk under the Act, so I drew hard lines: the bot shows progress to the new hire first, never scores people, asks before using anyone’s data, keeps a working path when someone declines, and cites a source for every factual answer.

08 · Key decisions

Three calls that shaped the agent

Decision 01

Meet people where they already work.

No new app, no portal. Syntea speaks first inside Microsoft Teams as a Copilot agent, using only what Teams and Copilot already offer: cards, suggested replies and prompt starters.

What it cost me: no canvas of my own. The whole interface is a message list, a few card layouts and a compose box, so the design work moved into structure, wording and timing.

There were a few ways to go. Here’s where I landed, and why.

I could have…A web dashboard for managersBut it’s one more tab to remember, and new hires never open it.
I could have…An onboarding checklist appBut the help ends when the checklist does. Nobody to ask afterwards.
My pickA Copilot agent inside Teams, built from native partsIt reaches managers and new hires where they already are, answers from company content, and it can speak first.

Decision 02

Ask first, then plan.

Syntea asks the manager before it prepares anything, and asks the new hire one question instead of nine. Everything else it already knows from the Teams profile.

Keeping it safe: AI used to evaluate employees can be high risk under the EU AI Act. So Syntea never scores people, shows new hires their own progress first, asks before using anyone’s data, and keeps a working path when someone declines.

There were a few ways to go. Here’s where I landed, and why.

I could have…Interview the new hire firstBut a nine field form in the first message asks for work before giving anything back.
I could have…Hand managers a six month spreadsheetBut it sends people out of Teams, and nobody wants six months on day one.
My pickAsk before acting, then draft from their own documentsManagers say yes first, review a draft built from their playbook and OKRs, and edit by message. New hires confirm what Teams already knows and answer one question.

Decision 03

Say so when it doesn’t know.

Every answer carries its source and a direct link. When there’s no confident answer, Syntea names the person who owns the topic, asks them for you, and remembers the reply for the next new hire.

When nobody’s around: if the expert doesn’t reply, Syntea follows up, then tells the new hire where things stand. From our story map: “If Kevin is not responsive, it will follow up multiple times.”

There were a few ways to go. Here’s where I landed, and why.

I could have…Always give a best guessBut one confident wrong answer about leave or pay ends the trust for good.
I could have…Reply “please contact HR”But that’s the dead end people came to the bot to avoid.
My pickName the owner, ask for you, follow upThe new hire gets a person, not a policy page, and the answer is stored for the next person who asks.

10 · Outcome

Onboardly stalled. Syntea didn’t.

In week 7, the client asked us to widen the scope back toward upskilling. We reframed the concept as an AI generated upskilling report, with consent prompts, a decline path and a career action plan. But expectations between the client and our team had drifted. Our evidence pointed at onboarding, the client’s priority leaned toward upskilling, and with some miscommunication on both sides the two directions never fully reconciled. With four weeks left, the onboarding track was put on hold, and the team regrouped around a new product of our own (a separate case study).

It felt like a failure at the time. In hindsight it was the most useful part of the project.

Later

Onboardly lived on as Syntea’s onboarding agent in Microsoft Teams: a light mode chat that greets the new hire in their first week, lays out today’s agenda, and points to the right video, podcast and people. Microsoft’s CEO showed it on stage in a keynote as an example of an agent built for Teams. It carries the bets we designed and tested in 2024: Teams native, proactive, personal, and grounded in the company’s own content.

11 · Learnings

What I’d keep, and what I’d do sooner

Write the problem statement with the client, not for them

Our Bull’s Eye was solid, but the client never co signed the problem statement. Next time I’d get one sentence signed off by the stakeholder by week 3 and revisit it in every Review.

In conversational UX, the first turn is the product

v1 was technically impressive and lost people in its first message. A bot earns the right to ask questions by giving value first.

Fake it before you build it

A human answering in Slack taught us what no prompt would have: answers need addresses, and people will ask a bot what they won’t ask a colleague.

Design for the moment the AI doesn’t know

Trust came from the honest hand off, not the clever answer. Repair and fallback paths are part of the happy path.

Say hi

I design for the moment people are unsure, and make the next step obvious.
